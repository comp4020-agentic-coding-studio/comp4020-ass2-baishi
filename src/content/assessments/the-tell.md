## The brief

> You are handed a sample. Decide whether it's real, and write the case a
> sceptical reader would actually need to be convinced.

Assignment 2 flips the semester's viewpoint from forger to detector: rather
than making a convincing fake, you're handed one (or a genuine sample —
you're not told which in advance) and asked to reach and defend a verdict.
The brief deliberately rewards the same honesty week 10's guest lecture
modelled: a confident wrong verdict is worse than an honest "probably, but
here's the one thing I couldn't rule out." Detection tooling gets you partway
there; naming what the tooling can't tell you is the rest of the mark.

## What you submit

A written case of 400–700 words: your verdict, the specific evidence for it
(quote a stroke-dynamics measurement, a spectral artefact, a stylometric
observation — whatever your assigned sample's material actually supports),
and an explicit statement of where your confidence is lowest. No separate
artefact beyond the write-up is required — the sample itself is provided in
the studio, not submitted back.
